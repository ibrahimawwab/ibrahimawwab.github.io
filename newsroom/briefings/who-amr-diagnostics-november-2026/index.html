WHO Sets 13 November AMR Diagnostics Briefing: What Laboratories Should Watch

WHO has scheduled a briefing on new AMR diagnostic resources and a decade of country-level progress reporting. Here is what the announcement means for microbiology laboratories—and what is not yet known.

Science briefingPublished 12 November 2026 at 00:57 (Afghanistan time)
Editorial disclosure: This is a self-published, source-based science briefing from Ibrahim Awwab's website. It summarises WHO's publicly announced agenda and explains relevant laboratory considerations. It is not a WHO statement, an interview, original clinical research or independently reported coverage of the author.

The World Health Organization has scheduled a press conference for 13 November 2026, 13:00–14:30 Central European Time, ahead of World AMR Awareness Week. According to WHO's published events programme, the session will cover new diagnostic resources for bacterial and fungal infections and antimicrobial resistance, along with findings from the tenth round of the Tracking AMR Country Self-assessment Survey (TrACSS).

The programme identifies two laboratory-focused resources: an essential package of diagnostic tests for bacterial and fungal infections and AMR, and a toolkit of standard operating procedures covering bacteriology, mycology and antimicrobial susceptibility testing. WHO also lists a dedicated launch webinar for these resources on 17 November 2026. As of this publication, the 13 November briefing has not taken place; its findings should not be reported as released results.

Why diagnostic capacity matters

Antimicrobial resistance is often discussed as a problem of drug development or prescribing, but reliable microbiology testing is also foundational. Culture and organism identification, appropriately validated susceptibility testing, quality management and reliable communication of results can help clinicians choose effective therapy and support surveillance programmes. Where laboratory access is uneven, both patient-level decisions and the completeness of resistance datasets can be affected.

WHO's July 2026 AMR fact sheet says that approximately one in six laboratory-confirmed bacterial infections worldwide in 2023 showed resistance to antibiotic treatment. This is a global estimate based on reported infections, not a claim about all infections or about current 2026 case numbers. Interpreting the figure requires attention to surveillance coverage, testing availability and differences among pathogens and regions.

Three questions laboratories should track

First, which tests are prioritised at each level of care? A tiered diagnostic package could help health systems plan which tests should be available locally and which need referral capacity. The precise package and implementation guidance should be read in WHO's primary release when published.

Second, how are standard operating procedures aligned with quality assurance? Bacteriology, mycology and susceptibility results depend on specimen quality, method validation, staff competency, appropriate controls and reliable reporting. Standardised procedures are useful only when paired with practical training, resourcing and oversight.

Third, how can surveillance data remain accurate and secure? Digital reporting can make laboratory findings more useful, but laboratories and health systems also need careful data governance, access controls, auditability and continuity planning. This last point is a technology and information-security consideration, not a claim that WHO announced new cybersecurity standards at this event.

Policy context and what comes next

In May 2026, the World Health Assembly adopted an updated Global Action Plan on antimicrobial resistance for 2026–2036. WHO describes it as a One Health framework that includes stronger laboratory networks, surveillance, prevention and appropriate antimicrobial use. The upcoming diagnostic events provide an opportunity to examine how global commitments translate into tools that laboratories can deploy and maintain.

After the 13 November WHO briefing and 17 November diagnostics launch, the next step for accurate coverage is to read the final releases and technical documents, distinguish promised resources from confirmed implementation, and avoid attributing preliminary interpretations to WHO.
